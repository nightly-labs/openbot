Why your team should write fewer meetings down

By Marta Kowalczyk · September 18, 2026 · 9 min read

Every team we have worked with keeps meeting notes. Few of them read those notes again. After a year of watching how twelve small companies take and use notes, we think most teams write down the wrong things, and too many of them.

The problem is not effort. The problem is that notes are written for an imagined reader who will never exist: a future colleague who wants to know what everybody said on a Tuesday in March. Real readers want something much smaller. They want to know what was decided, who owns the next step, and when it is due.

What people actually search for

We looked at six months of search logs from shared note tools. More than 70 percent of searches were for a person's name combined with a project name. Almost none were for a discussion topic. People search for commitments, not for conversations.

When we asked why, the answer was the same everywhere: "I want to know if I am blocked on somebody, or if somebody is blocked on me." A transcript does not answer that question quickly. A list of decisions does.

"We stopped taking minutes. We started writing three lines: decided, owner, date. Our Monday meeting went from an hour to twenty minutes." — operations lead at a 40-person logistics company

The three-line rule

The simplest format that worked was also the shortest. After each meeting, one person writes down every decision in one line, names one owner, and gives one date. If there was no decision, the note says so. That is useful information too: it tells you the meeting may not have been necessary.

Teams that adopted this rule reported fewer follow-up messages asking "what did we agree?" They also reported that new team members could catch up on a project in minutes instead of reading weeks of transcripts.

When long notes still help

There are exceptions. Incident reviews, legal discussions and design reviews with outside partners benefit from a detailed record. In those cases, write the long version, but put the three-line summary at the top so that most readers can stop there.

Recorded video is a special case. A recording feels complete, but nobody rewatches an hour of video to find one decision. If you record, still write the three lines, and link the timestamp where the decision was made.

How to start on Monday

Pick one recurring meeting. For the next four weeks, replace the usual notes with the three-line format. At the end, ask the team two questions: did you miss anything from the old notes, and did you read the new ones? In our experience, the answers are "no" and "yes".

Then do the same with the next meeting. You will find that some meetings produce no decisions at all, week after week. That is the moment to ask whether the meeting should become an email.

None of this needs a new tool. A shared document, a pinned message, or a ticket comment is enough. What matters is that the decisions are written in one place, in the same shape, every time.
